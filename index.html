Keeping a lab chamber at the right temperature, and letting it measure on its own

2026 Low Temperature Physics Lab · SRM University-AP View in Work Gallery ↗
↓

To study a material, we put a small sample in a vacuum chamber, heat it step by step, and measure it at each temperature. Our chamber kept overshooting the temperature it was told to reach. I found the setting that caused it, and wrote the software that now runs the whole measurement without anyone standing next to it.

The overshoot went from about 4 K to about 0.2 K. The setup works from room temperature up to 450 K (about 177 °C). Cooling with liquid nitrogen is planned but not running yet. I did this under my supervisor, Dr. Krishna Prasad Maity.

Technical details
  • Equipment: Janis ST-LN-500 probe station, Cryo-con 22C temperature controller, Wayne Kerr 6500B-series impedance analyser.
  • Machined an aluminium connector on a CNC, with a precision gasket, to carry electrical connections into the vacuum (10-2 mbar).
  • Wrote the temperature control first in LabVIEW, then in Python: an RS-232 driver, a control panel, and a staged ramp. A second program drives the impedance analyser over GPIB or LAN.
  • Together they step from 300 to 470 K in 5 K steps, wait 5 minutes within ±0.10 K, then run a 200-point sweep from 20 Hz to 10 MHz at each step. This produced the PMN-PT dataset below.
  • Overshoot study (5 Sep 2026): overshoot reduced from +4.08 K to +0.22 K over 297 to 450 K at 1.5 × 10-2 mbar. The usual explanation, "heat soak", did not hold: after the heater switched off, the temperature rose only 0.001 to 0.046 K more.
  • The cause was in the manual. The controller's IGAIN setting is an integral time in seconds, not a gain, so earlier tuning had gone the wrong way. A 30% heater-power cap also made the target unreachable. Final settings: P = 40, I = 900 s, maximum power 70%.
  • Safety cutoff: an Arduino with an optically isolated 4-channel relay board cuts the heater at 470 K. Outputs are set to the safe state before they are enabled, so no relay clicks when the board starts.

A faster light sensor made from two kinds of tiny crystals

2025 – 2026 Device Physics Lab · SRM University-AP View in Work Gallery ↗
↓

A light sensor made from two kinds of tiny crystals: cadmium selenide, and dots of graphene a few nanometres wide. Mixing them made the sensor respond about ten times faster. It works from ultraviolet, through visible light, into the near-infrared.

I built and automated the measurement setup, measured the samples and made the figures. I worked with Akshay Sampath and Nilja George, under Dr. Krishna Prasad Maity. Akshay and I have written a draft paper. It has not been submitted yet.

Technical details
  • Hydrothermal CdSe mixed with N,S-doped graphene quantum dots (GQD) at 2:1 and 3:1, drop-cast on silicon.
  • Photoresponse measured under 375, 530, 780 and 970 nm LEDs.
  • Rig automated in LabVIEW: Keithley 2636B source meter, Thorlabs DC2200 LED driver, Zurich Instruments MFLI lock-in amplifier, OMM-6810B optical power meter.
  • LED power calibrated with my own Python scripts over GPIB. LED and sample holder designed and 3D-printed, so every measurement uses the same geometry.
  • Results: responsivity 0.098 A/W, external quantum efficiency 32.6%, specific detectivity 1.5 × 1011 Jones, linear dynamic range 14.9 dB, rise time 200 ms down to 20 ms, lock-in tracking up to 50 Hz.

Can a magnet change how a material responds to light?

Jan – Apr 2026 Device Physics Lab · SRM University-AP UG/PG SEED Grant
↓

The university gave us a small research grant to test one idea. We took cadmium selenide, added a little chromium to make it weakly magnetic, and measured the current it gives under light, with and without a magnetic field.

The data is collected. There is no report or paper yet. I worked with Visitha Padilam, under Dr. Krishna Prasad Maity.

Technical details
  • Samples: pristine CdSe and 3%, 5% and 7% Cr-doped CdSe (a dilute magnetic semiconductor).
  • I-V sweeps in the dark and under a 530 nm LED (5 to 50 mA drive), with an electromagnet at 0 to 5 A.
  • Calibrated the electromagnet: 0.2 to 7.8 A gives 181 to 5,260 gauss.
  • Recorded with the same LabVIEW rig as the light sensor above. Wrote a small Streamlit tool to analyse the sweeps.

How a ceramic stores electric charge as it gets hotter

Sep 2026 Device Physics Lab · SRM University-AP
↓

PMN-PT is a ceramic used in sensors and actuators. I measured how well it stores electric charge from room temperature up to 460 K. This was the first full job for the automated setup described above: it ran the whole measurement by itself.

Technical details
  • Temperature-dependent dielectric spectroscopy: 20 Hz to 10 MHz at 100 mV, 300 to 460 K, on the probe station. Heating and cooling runs in air and in vacuum.
  • Real permittivity computed from the sample geometry (0.30 mm thick, 6 mm2).
  • Result: permittivity peaks near 460 K, with a maximum of about 877 at 1 kHz.
  • Earlier, with Visitha Padilam, I modelled equivalent circuits in ZView (Apr 2026).

Checking what a thin film is really made of

Sep – Nov 2026 SRM University-AP
↓

Molybdenum disulphide is a layered material that people study for future electronics. A thin film of it was grown in a furnace. I examined the film with X-rays and with light to confirm what it was, and wrote three reports.

Technical details
  • MoS2 grown by chemical vapour deposition from MoO3 and sulphur in argon, on Si/SiO2.
  • XRD with Scherrer and Williamson-Hall analysis: 9 of 13 peaks assigned to 2H-MoS2, mean crystallite size about 48 nm.
  • XPS peak fitting for Mo 3d, S 2p, O 1s and C 1s. UV spectroscopy with a Tauc plot for the band gap.
  • I made the figures myself in Origin and wrote the reports in LaTeX.

↓

Electrospinning uses a high voltage to pull a plastic solution into very fine fibres. I made fibres from three plastics and studied them under an electron microscope. One part did not work: the fibres that were meant to carry silver showed no silver.

Technical details

Designing superconducting qubits on a computer

Aug 2026 Self-Project
↓

A qubit is the basic part of a quantum computer. One common kind is a tiny superconducting circuit on a chip. I learned the software used to design these chips, drew a single qubit and then a pair of connected qubits, and simulated how they would behave. These are designs on a computer. Nothing has been fabricated.

Technical details
  • Keysight ADS 2026 with QuantumPro. I followed Keysight's tutorial and then extended it.
  • Single transmon with a readout resonator: qubit about 6.55 GHz, anharmonicity about 361 MHz, resonator about 8.16 GHz, dispersive shift about 7.0 MHz.
  • Two transmons with a bus coupler: qubits at about 6.61 and 6.72 GHz, coupling J about 6.25 MHz, shared resonator at about 8.58 GHz.
  • Layout exported to GDSII with 0 errors. Niobium response plotted at 300 K and 9 K.
  • All figures are simulation results, not measurements.

Oberleaf: a free editor for writing scientific documents on your own computer

Sep 2026 – Present Self-Project Visit ↗
↓

Most scientists write papers and reports in a system called LaTeX. Online editors put a limit on how long a document may take to build, and a long thesis can hit it. Oberleaf does the work on your own computer, so there is no limit. When something goes wrong, it explains the error in plain English. I write my own CV and lab reports in it.

Technical details
  • Open source (MIT licence). Not affiliated with Overleaf.
  • Recompiles in 1.3 to 1.8 s on an i7-1165G7 laptop. Equation preview in under a millisecond on a 10-page document.
  • Explains 52 common TeX and BibTeX errors, with one-click fixes for 27 of them.
  • Jump between source and PDF, outline, review comments, a saved checkpoint on every compile, whole-project import from Overleaf, automatic updates.

Portfolio Insight: a private tracker for investors in the Nepal stock market

I also built a score that ranks stocks, and then tested honestly whether it works. It did not beat the market, and I report it that way.

Technical details

Quantum Double Shield Protocol: sharing a secret key and noticing if someone listens

Aug 2025 Hackathon Visit ↗
↓

Today's internet encryption depends on the fact that ordinary computers cannot split very large numbers into their factors. A large enough quantum computer could. Our team set out to show a way of sharing a secret key that stays safe: anyone who listens in disturbs the signal, and can be detected.

We built it at the Amaravati Quantum Valley Hackathon, where it placed third.

Technical details
  • BB84 quantum key distribution combined with a quantum random number generator, simulated in Qiskit, with eavesdropper detection by error rate.

Why quantum computers threaten today's encryption (course paper)

Nov 2024 Academic-Project
↓

Every message we send online, a WhatsApp text, a bank transaction, an email, is protected by encryption. Most of it relies on one simple fact: multiplying 3 × 5 = 15 is easy, but given a number hundreds of digits long, finding the two numbers it came from is extremely hard for an ordinary computer. In this course paper I studied how Shor's algorithm breaks that assumption, and the new kinds of encryption being built to resist it.

Supervised by Dr. Sazzad Ali Biswas.

How I Think

  1. Believe in the power of compounding.

    In learning, in relationships and in investing. I rise early and keep a routine, because small things done every day add up. I invest in the Nepal stock market the same way, for the long term.

  2. Think from fundamentals, and build it by hand.

    Physics taught me to question anything in depth. I understand a thing properly only after I have built it. When the lab's temperature controller misbehaved, I measured it, read the manual again, and found the real cause.

  3. A tool should explain itself.

    I learned this from Don Norman's book on design. It is why Oberleaf explains errors in plain English, and why this page starts every entry in ordinary words.

  4. Build what does not exist yet.

    Most of my projects began with something missing around me: a calculator my mom could use for village loans, a place for students on my campus to find a mentor.

  5. Give credit by name.

    Almost nothing here was done alone. I try to name the people who did the work, as I did in my article on Qiskit Fall Fest.

In the long run I want to build a business that funds scientists, and to bring quantum technology to Nepal.
